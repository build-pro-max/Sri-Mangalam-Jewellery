Reading the motifs on temple jewellery

Lakshmi kasu, mango malai, peacocks — what the old designs are actually saying.

Temple jewellery began as ornament for deities and for the dancers who performed before them. That is why the pieces are frontal, bold and legible from a distance — they were made to be read across a hall, not admired at conversation range. Every motif on them carries a meaning, and once you can read a few, choosing a piece becomes much easier.

Kasu malai — the coin chain

A row of small stamped discs, each bearing Lakshmi seated on a lotus. Historically these were real coins strung together, a family holding its wealth in a wearable form. On a modern kasu malai the coins are struck in gold sheet and given a matte antique finish. More rows means more weight and more presence; a single row is a piece a bride can wear again on ordinary festival days.

Manga malai — the mango

The paisley shape that repeats in graduated sizes is a mango, not a teardrop. Mango means fertility, sweetness and abundance across South Indian art, which is why the manga malai is a wedding piece above all. Look at how the sizes step down from the centre: on a well-made malai the gradation is smooth and the largest mango sits exactly at the sternum.

Peacocks, swans and the goddess

  • Peacock (mayil). Rain, beauty and Murugan. Usually placed in pairs, facing each other around a central stone.
  • Swan (annam). Discernment — the bird said to separate milk from water. Common on jhumka tops and side pieces.
  • Lakshmi. Prosperity, and the reason so many pendants are built around a seated figure flanked by elephants or lamps.

Nakshi, and why the finish is matte

Nakshi work is raised from behind — the karigar hammers the design outward from the reverse of a thin gold sheet, then chases the detail in from the front. The surface is left deliberately unpolished so that the raised figures read as light and shadow rather than as glare. A mirror polish on a temple piece flattens all that work, which is why we ask customers not to have antique pieces buffed at a roadside polisher.

The old finishes were made for oil-lamp light. They still look best in warm light, not under a white tube light.

Red and green stones

The traditional pairing is ruby red with emerald green, set in closed gold cups rather than claws. Most temple pieces today use synthetic or semi-precious stones of those colours, which is normal and honestly priced — what matters is that the cups are properly closed and the stones sit level. Ask, and we will tell you exactly what is set in the piece you are holding.

Buying a temple piece to keep

Weight and honest handwork are what survive fashion. Look at the back of the piece: neat, finished reverse work and evenly soldered joints are the signs of a piece your daughter will still want to wear.
